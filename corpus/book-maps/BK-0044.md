## Central thesis

Human error is neither random nor simply the fault of the person at the sharp end: errors take systematic forms that are predictable from how the mind ordinarily works, and the same cognitive machinery produces both competent performance and characteristic error. The book's most consequential move is to separate active errors — the immediate, sharp-end failures whose effects are felt at once — from latent errors, the decisions and conditions seeded earlier by designers, builders, and managers that lie dormant in a system until they combine with active failures and a triggering event to breach the system's defences. On this account, major systems disasters are better explained by latent errors and weakened defences than by front-line error alone, and error management should shift from individual blame toward systemic conditions and defences. (Locators: Chapter 1; Chapter 3; Chapter 7.)

## Argument structure

- **Error is defined relative to intention.** An error is a planned sequence of actions that fails to achieve its intended outcome; this framing is what makes error analysable. (Chapter 1.)
- **Errors fall into systematic types tied to a performance level.** Skill-based slips and lapses, rule-based mistakes, and knowledge-based mistakes are distinct error types, not one undifferentiated "human error." (Chapter 3.)
- **Error forms are predictable from cognitive under-specification.** When processing is under-specified, the mind falls back on similarity-matching and frequency-gambling, which produces recurring, describable error forms. (Chapter 4.)
- **One cognitive architecture explains both correct and errorful performance.** The Generic Error-Modelling System models how the same machinery yields competence and characteristic error. (Chapter 5.)
- **Major systems disasters arise from latent errors breaching defences.** Latent errors combine with active failures and a trigger to defeat a system's defences; the sharp-end operator is rarely the whole story. (Chapter 7.)
- **Reducing error risk means assessing and strengthening systemic defences.** Error-risk work addresses the system and its defences, not only the correction of the front-line person. (Chapter 8.)
- **The book reframes error from individual fault to systemic condition.** Its overall stance moves the unit of analysis from the erring person to the conditions and defences around them. *(Map inference about the book's framing.)*

## Misuse risks

- **Named-model-as-present.** *Human Error* (1990) predates Reason's named Swiss-cheese model; that named metaphor does not appear in this source. Attributing the named model to this book misdates the lineage — this book carries the latent/active distinction, the resident-pathogen account, and the defences foundations, not the later named model.
- **Latent-error-as-vague-label.** "Latent error" used loosely for any background factor, decoupled from the specific dormant-until-activated mechanism the book describes.
- **Defences-read-as-add-more-barriers.** Defence-in-depth reduced to "add more checks and barriers", without engaging how defences are themselves eroded, bypassed, or breached.
- **Individual-blame inversion.** The framework used to pin fault on the front-line operator (the active error) when the book's emphasis is the latent contributions upstream of the sharp end.
- **Error-type taxonomy as rigid checklist.** The skill-, rule-, and knowledge-based scheme run as a checkbox classification that loses the boundary and mixed cases.
- **Universalising the cognitive model.** GEMS or the error heuristics imported as a complete theory of cognition rather than an error-focused model.
- **Conflating Reason with Perrow or Vaughan.** The cognitive-and-defences account treated as interchangeable with structural-coupling (Perrow) or cultural-normalization (Vaughan) accounts of disaster.
