## Central thesis

Strategy is not a goal, a vision, or a planning ritual. It is a coherent response to a specific challenge that names what is hard, picks a way to act on what is hard, and aligns concrete moves behind that approach. Most artifacts called "strategy" omit one or more of those three elements and substitute aspirational language, broad goals, or unconnected action lists. The book argues that the difference between good and bad strategy is structural rather than stylistic, and offers a kernel (diagnosis, guiding policy, coherent action) that is both how to construct strategy and how to detect its absence. (Locators: Introduction; Chapter 5; Chapter 3.)

## Argument structure

- **Bad strategy is recognizable.** Its four hallmarks — fluff, failure to face the challenge, mistaking goals for strategy, and bad strategic objectives — are diagnostic, not merely critical. (Chapter 3; Chapter 4.)
- **Good strategy has a kernel.** Diagnosis, guiding policy, coherent action — not as a template but as the minimum structural requirement. (Chapter 5.)
- **Power comes from concentration.** Leverage, proximate objectives, and focus are not motivational devices; they are how a finite organization gets disproportionate effect. (Chapter 6; Chapter 7; Chapter 10.)
- **Coherence is a system property.** Chain-link systems and design-based thinking treat the strategy as an arrangement of mutually-reinforcing actions, not a list. (Chapter 8; Chapter 9.)
- **Dynamics matter more than position.** The advantage and dynamics chapters argue that the question is not "where are we" but "what is shifting and how do we ride it." (Chapter 12; Chapter 13.)
- **Inertia and entropy are the default.** Organizations decay toward routine; strategy work is in part a struggle against that decay. (Chapter 14.)
- **The strategist's discipline is composition and conjecture.** Putting the kernel together is an act of design under uncertainty, not a deduction from data. (Chapter 15; Chapter 16; Chapter 17; Chapter 18.)

## Misuse risks

- **Diagnosis becomes theater.** Operators (or AI advice) produce a fluent-sounding "diagnosis" paragraph that does not actually name the challenge, and call the result a strategy.
- **"Bad strategy" becomes a vague insult.** The four hallmarks degrade into a label thrown at any plan the speaker dislikes, decoupled from the structural test.
- **The book is treated as universally applicable.** The text is anchored in large-organization examples; transferring the kernel into 2-person product decisions without adaptation produces ceremony, not strategy.
- **Strategy gets flattened into planning or execution cadence.** "We do quarterly OKRs" gets called strategy work; the book's discipline is the opposite of that move.
- **Over-weighting a famous strategy source.** Because the book is famous, model output may anchor on it and ignore the contradictions named above (Mintzberg, Porter, Lafley, Ries).
- **The kernel becomes a checklist.** "Diagnosis / guiding policy / coherent action" headings on a slide are treated as the work, instead of the structural minimum a real strategy must contain.
- **"Focus" gets read as productivity advice.** Concentration against the named challenge is replaced with generic "do less" sentiments untied to the diagnosis.
