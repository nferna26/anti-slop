## Claim

A systems disaster is produced by a mechanism with several parts, not by a single front-line error. Reason separates two kinds of error. Active errors are the failures of the people at a system's controls — pilots, operators, controllers — and they take effect at once. Latent errors are introduced earlier and further from the controls, by designers, managers, maintainers, and senior decision-makers; they do no damage by themselves and can persist, unexpressed, until other factors bring them into play. A disaster occurs when latent errors, an active error, and a triggering event line up and defeat the layers of defence engineered into the system — the redundancies, automatic safety devices, and physical barriers a high-hazard system is given to stop exactly such a sequence. Because the latent contributions are usually in place well before the sharp-end error, the front-line operator has most often been handed a system whose flaws were already present, rather than being the origin of the failure. The unit of analysis the account points to is therefore the whole arrangement of latent errors and defences, not the operator's error alone.

## Paraphrase

Chapter 7 of the local BK-0044 source argues that the human contribution to a systems disaster cannot be read off the front-line operator alone. It opens by separating active errors — the immediate failures of the people at a system's controls — from latent errors, which are seeded earlier and at a distance by designers, builders, managers, maintainers, and high-level decision-makers, and which can sit unexpressed in a system until conditions bring them together. Reason's claim is that latent errors, not active ones, are what most endangers a complex system: the operator at the sharp end is usually working inside a system whose flaws were built in well before, and a disaster needs a set of contributing factors — latent errors, an active error, and a trigger — no one of which would be enough by itself, converging and getting past the defences engineered into the system. Those defences are themselves described: modern high-hazard systems are built on the principle of defence-in-depth — multiple independent layers of protection — and a catastrophe is what happens when that layered protection is defeated. The chapter grounds this in six major accidents and offers a resident-pathogen metaphor — latent errors as inert pathogens that do harm only when other factors activate them. Its closing postscript adds a caution against retrospective blame: hindsight makes the operators' failures look obvious in a way they were not at the time, which is itself a reason not to stop the analysis at the sharp-end error.

## Scope conditions

The mechanism is described for complex, high-hazard technological systems — nuclear power, chemical process plants, aviation, marine transport — that are built with engineered defence-in-depth; Chapter 7's evidence is six such disasters. It is an account of how disasters in such systems are produced, not a claim that every failure has latent errors behind it, nor that managing latent errors alone prevents disaster — Chapter 7, drawing on Perrow, allows that some complex, tightly-coupled systems may suffer breakdowns that are not foreseeable. The card uses the source's own terms — "active errors", "latent errors" (which the source also calls "latent failures"), "defences", "defence-in-depth", and the "resident pathogen" metaphor — and not Reason's later relabellings ("latent conditions") or his later named model. The BK-0044 acquisition registry records `locator_confidence: medium`; the Chapter 7 boundary used by this card has been confirmed against the local source. The defence-strengthening and error-risk-reduction response is the subject of Chapter 8 and is a separate candidate card, not carded here.

## Misuse risk

Using "latent error" as a vague label for any background factor, decoupled from the specific dormant-until-activated-by-other-factors mechanism the chapter describes. A second misuse is reading the account as fully exonerating the front-line operator: Chapter 7 situates the active error inside a flawed system, but it does not erase the operator's contribution — it relocates the centre of gravity, it does not abolish individual action. A third misuse is attributing the named Swiss-cheese model to this source: *Human Error* (1990) predates that named model and uses the resident-pathogen metaphor instead; the named model must not be read back into this 1990 text.
