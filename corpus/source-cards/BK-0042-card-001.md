## Claim

Normalization of deviance is the organizational process by which a group that repeatedly encounters warning signs — conditions or performance that depart from what was expected — comes to treat each departure as acceptable, so that the threshold between an acceptable condition and a dangerous one moves outward one incident at a time. Each anomaly is examined, reconciled with the group's existing understanding, and recorded as a tolerable risk; the next, larger anomaly is then judged against the threshold that has already moved. Over a long run this lets an organization operate a steadily more degraded condition while continuing to regard it as routine and safe. Vaughan presents this as a general account of how organizational disasters incubate — not as something peculiar to the Challenger case — and locates its drivers in the working group's own evolving culture, the wider organisational and professional culture that surrounds it, and structural secrecy: the bureaucratic partitioning of information that keeps separate warning signs from being assembled into one recognized trend. The outcome can be catastrophic even though no rule is broken and no participant sets out to cause harm.

## Paraphrase

Chapter 10 of the local BK-0042 source is the book's theoretical chapter: it lifts the Challenger case into a general account of how organizational disasters take shape. Vaughan's argument is that the launch decision was not the work of managers knowingly trading safety against schedule; the mistake was socially produced — it grew out of how the organization ordinarily and unremarkably worked, not out of individual wrongdoing. The chapter builds the explanation from three combined factors: the shared technical worldview and working norms a group develops as it repeatedly judges the same problem; the surrounding organisational and professional culture that made conforming to cost, schedule, and bureaucratic expectations feel normal; and structural secrecy — the way information was partitioned across the bureaucracy so that warning signs stayed faint, inconsistent, and scattered instead of adding up to a recognized trend. The core of the chapter is that, through this combination, a group facing repeated indications of danger reinterprets each one as within bounds; the threshold for what counts as acceptable then moves to admit the next incident, and the one after that, so that an increasingly degraded condition keeps being certified as safe to continue. The chapter is explicit that catastrophe did not require a broken rule or anyone meaning to cause harm. It also extends the pattern beyond NASA, pointing to other organizations — including smaller and less technical ones — where a comparable widening of accepted risk preceded serious failures, and it warns that the process can look reasonable, even useful, from inside an organization even as it moves toward disaster.

## Scope conditions

The mechanism presumes an organization that meets the same hazard repeatedly, runs a formal risk-assessment or review process, and has the bureaucratic structure that produces structural secrecy. Chapter 10 explicitly generalises the pattern beyond NASA — it points to disasters in other, smaller, and less technical organizations — so the claim is not confined to large technical bureaucracies; but the chapter is equally explicit that the process depends on those preconditions, so the concept does not apply to every safety lapse. The carded claim explains how deviance becomes normalized; it does not, on its own, establish that every accident involves normalization of deviance. The BK-0042 acquisition registry records `locator_confidence: verified`; the Chapter 10 page range (pp. 404–439) used by this card has been confirmed against the local source. The chapter's separate transfer claim — whether the wider framework applies cleanly to other safety-critical bureaucracies — is listed as its own candidate card in the BK-0042 book map and is not carded here.

## Misuse risk

Invoking "normalization of deviance" as a vague label for any safety degradation without showing that its preconditions hold — repeated danger signals, an institutional risk-acceptance process, and the structural partitioning of information. A second misuse is reading "structural secrecy" as deliberate concealment, which inverts the claim: the chapter presents it as an emergent property of bureaucratic structure, not a conscious act. A third is treating the account as exonerating individuals; the chapter situates individual responsibility inside a structural pattern, it does not erase it.
