## Claim

Chapter 3 of *High Output Management* advances the proposition that a manager's output is not the manager's own activity but the output of the organisational units the manager is responsible for — the unit directly supervised, together with the neighbouring units the manager influences without supervising. Grove draws a firm line between *activity* — what a manager personally does — and *output*, the result those activities exist to produce, and places a manager's output entirely in the latter, measured at the level of the group rather than the individual. A manager can be personally effective at their own tasks, and that, by this definition, is still not their output. The claim deliberately widens "manager" to include know-how specialists who hold no reporting line but shape other units' work through expertise.

## Paraphrase

Chapter 3 opens by asking what a manager's output actually is, and rejects a list of managerial activities — judgments, direction, resource allocation, mistake-detection, developing people — as the answer: those are things a manager does to create a result, not the result itself. The output is the completed work of the group: what the unit the manager runs produces, together with what the units the manager influences produce. Grove's reasoning is that the work is carried out by teams, so a manager's contribution is measured by what the team produces — the way a win is credited to a team, not to one player.

## Scope conditions

The claim concerns how to define and locate a manager's output — the unit of account — not a measurement formula and not a performance-appraisal procedure (appraisal is a later chapter). Chapter 3 applies the definition across deliberately varied settings — a manufacturing plant, a design group, a school principal, a surgeon — so it is not industry-specific; but it is a definitional reframing, not an empirical finding, and states what to *count* as a manager's output, not how large or how good that output is. The card carries only the manager's-output definition; managerial leverage — that activities differ widely in output per unit of effort — is a separate candidate card. *High Output Management* (the 2015 Vintage edition; originally 1983) predates much present-day distributed and knowledge-work organisation; the card records what Chapter 3 contains and does not extend it. The Chapter 3 boundary is confirmed against the local source; `locator_confidence: medium`.

## Misuse risk

The main misuse is reading "a manager's output is the team's output" as licence for the manager to disengage — treating personal work as unnecessary. Chapter 3's claim runs the other way: personal activity is what creates the output and is necessary; it simply is not *itself* the output. A second misuse is collapsing the definition into an accounting formula that assigns a unit's whole result to its manager and ignores everything else bearing on that result. A third is over-extension: treating the definition as a claim about how large or how good a manager's output is, when it speaks only to what should be *counted*.
