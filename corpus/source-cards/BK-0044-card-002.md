## Claim

Chapter 8 is the closing chapter of *Human Error*, and it sets out Reason's response to the human-error risk in hazardous systems. Its premise is that error cannot be designed away: because most errors fall into systematic patterns produced by the same cognitive processes that ordinarily serve people well, no one technique for reducing error works across all settings and the elimination of error is not an attainable goal. The response Chapter 8 argues for is therefore to raise a hazardous system's tolerance of the errors that will occur — shaping equipment and procedures so they invite fewer errors and make errors easy to detect and reverse, supplying the people who run and maintain the system with aids for their known cognitive limits, and engineering defences and back-up redundancies so that a single human or component failure cannot by itself propagate to catastrophe — with remedial effort aimed at limiting what an error can do rather than at preventing every error. Chapter 8 also fixes that response's boundary: the engineered defences built into these systems have worked well enough that complex technologies can now withstand almost any single human or component failure, which Reason treats as a genuine achievement, but they do not reach the latent failures that accumulate in the management and structure of any large organisation — a residual hazard the chapter says technology cannot remove — so Reason concludes that work on error must extend from individual fallibility to the organisational level.

## Paraphrase

Chapter 8 is the closing chapter of the local BK-0044 source, and it turns from the book's largely theoretical earlier chapters to the question of remedies. Its first half surveys how the human contribution to risk is assessed — probabilistic risk assessment, which models a plant as logical fault and event trees, and the family of human reliability analysis techniques built to feed human-error numbers into those models. Reason's reading of those techniques is sceptical: he reports that they are largely unvalidated, that different analysts applying the same method can produce estimates that differ by orders of magnitude, and that the methods are too simplistic to be taken as a true picture of how operators behave — assessment matters, in his account, because sound assessment ought to drive effective error reduction, but the tools as they stand are weak. The chapter's second half, on reducing the error risk, starts from a firm premise: the human-error risk is not a stock of random faults that better tools or more training can drain away, because most errors fall into systematic patterns that are a by-product of the very cognitive processes that usually serve people well — so no one technique for reducing error works across all settings, and eliminating error is not an attainable goal. From that premise Reason gathers a set of remedial measures: shaping equipment and procedures so their physical and informational form invites fewer errors and makes errors easy to notice and to undo; giving operators and maintainers decision aids and memory aids that compensate for known cognitive limits; interface design whose stated aim is not to remove error but to raise the system's tolerance of it; and training that lets people make and recover from errors rather than drilling errors out. Under the list sits one strategy — since errors will happen, remedial effort is best spent limiting their consequences, and on engineered defences and back-up redundancies that keep a single failure from propagating to catastrophe, rather than spent chasing the elimination of error. The chapter's epilogue then states the response's boundary plainly: the engineered defences built into these systems have worked well enough that complex technologies can now withstand almost any single human or component failure — Reason calls this a real achievement — but those defences do not remove the hazard that remains, the slow build-up of latent failures in the management and structure of any large organisation, a hazard the chapter says technology cannot remedy. Chapter 8 closes by arguing that the study of human error must widen from individual fallibility to the fallibility of organisations, because it is now the organisational and institutional level, more than the individual one, that most threatens the safety of complex systems.

## Scope conditions

The response is described for complex, high-hazard technological systems — Chapter 8's running examples are nuclear power generation, chemical process plants, aviation, and marine transport — that are built with engineered defence-in-depth and run under formal risk assessment. It is an account of how the human-error risk in such systems is best reduced; it is not a claim that error-tolerant design and engineered defences prevent all disasters, and Chapter 8 is explicit about the response's limit — engineered defences address single failures but, by Reason's own statement, do not reach latent failure at the managerial and organisational level, which the chapter says technology cannot remove. The card uses the source's own terms — "error tolerance", "defence-in-depth", "redundancies", "latent failures", "probabilistic risk assessment", "human reliability analysis", and Norman's "affordances" — and not Reason's later relabellings or his later named model. The BK-0044 acquisition registry records `locator_confidence: medium`; the Chapter 8 boundary used by this card has been confirmed against the local source (Chapter 8 is the book's final chapter and is followed by the Appendix of case studies). *Human Error* (1990) predates Reason's named Swiss-cheese model; this card describes Chapter 8's error-tolerance-and-defence response in the book's own 1990 vocabulary and does not attribute that named model to this source.

## Misuse risk

Reading Chapter 8 as an endorsement of "add more defences, more procedure, more checks" as a complete answer to a systems disaster. The chapter's response is error tolerance, and the chapter states a boundary on it that this misuse erases: engineered defences, however effective against single failures, do not by themselves reach the latent organisational failures behind major disasters. A second misuse is reading the response as straightforwardly optimistic — Chapter 8 is sceptical that the error-elimination project can succeed and sceptical of the human-reliability-assessment tools meant to support it; it is a measured response, not a promise that the error risk can be engineered away. A third misuse is treating the error-tolerance idea as licence to accept a known degraded condition: Chapter 8 argues for limiting the consequences of the errors that occur, not for tolerating hazards that could be removed.
