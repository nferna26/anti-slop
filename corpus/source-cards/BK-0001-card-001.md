## Claim

Good strategy has a definite structure that Rumelt calls the kernel, and the kernel has three elements: a diagnosis that names the nature of the challenge by identifying which aspects of a complex situation are critical; a guiding policy that chooses an overall approach for dealing with the challenge named in the diagnosis; and a set of coherent actions — concrete, coordinated, mutually reinforcing steps — that carry out the guiding policy. The kernel is the structural minimum a real strategy must contain. An artifact that states goals, offers aspirational language, or lists unconnected actions, but lacks one or more of the three elements, is not a strategy. The same three-element structure therefore serves both to construct a strategy and to detect its absence.

## Paraphrase

Chapter 5 of the local BK-0001 source argues that most artifacts called strategy lack the structure of one, and names the structure they should have: the kernel. The first element, diagnosis, replaces the overwhelming complexity of a situation with a simpler account that identifies what is critical; the chapter stresses that a diagnosis is a judgment about the meaning of facts, not a fact that can be proven, and that deep strategic change usually begins with a changed diagnosis. The second element, guiding policy, is an overall approach chosen to cope with the challenge the diagnosis named; the chapter is explicit that a guiding policy is not a goal, a vision, or a desired end state — it is a method of grappling with the situation that rules out a wide range of possible actions. The third element, coherent action, is a set of concrete steps that carry out the guiding policy and that are coordinated so they reinforce rather than conflict with one another; the chapter contrasts this with action that conflicts internally and with a list of unrelated actions that each require senior approval but do not address one challenge together — which, the chapter says, does not constitute a strategy. The kernel is presented as the bare-bones centre of a strategy: a strategy may contain more, but if the kernel is absent or misshapen there is a serious problem.

## Scope conditions

Applies wherever there is a genuine strategic challenge — an ill-structured problem where the situation must be interpreted before action. Chapter 5 applies the kernel across very different settings (a clinical case, foreign policy, a large company, a small owner-run shop), so the claim is not confined to large organisations; but the kernel is a *necessary* structure, not a *sufficient* one. A strategy can contain all three elements and still be bad if the diagnosis is mistaken — the kernel tests for the presence of strategy, not for its quality. The deeper Chapter 5 sub-claims (that a guiding policy is a chosen approach rather than a statement of intent; that coherent action means mutually reinforcing moves rather than a list) are listed as separate candidate cards in the BK-0001 book map and are not carded here. ## Misuse risk

Rendering the kernel as three slide headings — Diagnosis, Guiding Policy, Coherent Action — and treating the headings as the work, rather than as a structural minimum that each demand real judgment. A second misuse is the inverse: using "that is not a real strategy, it has no kernel" as a rhetorical dismissal of any plan without honestly applying the three-element test. The card's claim is structural and specific; the misuse in both directions is to invoke the kernel as a label without doing the diagnosis-policy-action analysis it names.
