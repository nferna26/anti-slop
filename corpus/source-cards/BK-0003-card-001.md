## Claim

Bryar and Carr present Working Backwards as a product-development method in which a team writes the customer-facing announcement and the accompanying FAQ before committing to build. The artifact is not treated as launch copy. It is a decision instrument: the announcement has to make the proposed customer benefit concrete enough to test, while the FAQ has to surface the practical questions that would otherwise remain hidden until implementation - market size, economics, dependencies, feasibility, operating risk, and unresolved constraints. A PR/FAQ that cannot survive that review can stop or redirect the effort before engineering and product resources are spent; a PR/FAQ that does survive gives leaders and the team a clearer account of what would have to be true for the product to be worth building.

## Paraphrase

Chapter 5 argues that Amazon's product-development method changed when teams stopped beginning with internal opportunity logic - market size, financial projections, technology plans, or feature lists - and instead had to write a document as if the customer-facing product already existed. The press-release portion makes the team describe the customer, the problem, and the proposed experience in compact public language. That constraint matters because a vague or internally convenient idea has less room to hide when it has to be stated as a concrete customer benefit. The FAQ portion then moves from promise to scrutiny: external questions test whether customers would understand and value the product, while internal questions force the team to face economics, required staffing, technical feasibility, legal and partner dependencies, risks, and the conditions under which the idea would or would not be viable.

The chapter's Kindle account shows the contrast. Before the press-release approach, the digital-media effort was framed around Amazon's business opportunity, technology work, financial models, and marketing possibilities; after the team wrote from the customer's future experience, the problem shifted toward what would make reading, buying, downloading, selection, and pricing compelling for customers. The chapter's fictional "Melinda" example makes the method's filtering role more explicit: a PR/FAQ can reveal that a product idea is not worth pursuing because the addressable market is too small, the usage burden is too high, the dependencies are too fragile, or the cost structure does not work. In that sense, the artifact is valuable even when it kills the idea. It lets the team and leaders learn what must be solved before major resources are committed, and it preserves the option to stop, revise, wait, partner, acquire, or accept a risk knowingly.

The carded claim is the method claim: PR/FAQ is a pre-build artifact for clarifying customer value and constraints before investment. The chapter also contains narrower sub-claims about document format, meeting process, revision cadence, Kindle, AWS, and the sample PR/FAQ components; those are left for separate cards.

## Scope conditions

The claim applies to product, service, policy, or initiative decisions where the team can write a plausible future customer or stakeholder outcome before building, and where leaders can still change the investment decision. Chapter 5 is strongest for new-product work: it repeatedly focuses on products and services, and its detailed examples revolve around customer-facing offerings. The chapter says the method can also be used for other initiatives, but this card does not generalise the method to every strategic or operational decision. It also does not claim that writing a PR/FAQ is sufficient evidence that customers will want the product; the document sharpens assumptions and constraints, but it is still an artifact produced before launch. The carded claim is separate from BK-0003's six-pager method (Chapter 4), input-metrics method (Chapter 6), and Part-2 case-study claims. ## Misuse risk

Turning PR/FAQ into ceremony: a team writes a crisp customer-facing announcement after the real decision has already been made, then treats the document as validation rather than as a way to expose whether the idea should proceed. A second misuse is confusing the artifact with customer evidence; a polished PR/FAQ can make an imagined future feel real without proving that customers will behave as expected. A third misuse is importing the Amazon artifact without the review discipline around it - multiple revisions, senior review, hard questions, and the willingness to stop a product that does not survive scrutiny.
