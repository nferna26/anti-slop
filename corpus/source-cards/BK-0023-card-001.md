## Claim

Chapter 1 of *Competitive Strategy* presents five competitive forces as the mechanism that sets how profitable an industry can be. Porter's claim is that how hard firms compete in an industry — and so the return the industry can sustain over the long run — is governed not by coincidence and not by the conduct of current rivals alone, but by five structural forces taken together: the threat of new entry, the threat of substitute products, the bargaining power of buyers, the bargaining power of suppliers, and rivalry among existing competitors. It is the *combined* strength of all five that fixes the ceiling on the industry's long-run profitability — its return on invested capital; industries differ fundamentally in how profitable they can be because the combined strength of their forces differs. Because that competition grows out of the industry's own economics, the durable structure is held apart from short-run factors that move profitability only transiently. The most powerful one or two of the forces dominate, and analysing all five is offered as where the work of building a competitive strategy begins.

## Paraphrase

Chapter 1 of the local BK-0023 source argues that how fiercely firms compete in an industry, and so how much profit they can sustain, is set by the industry's economics rather than by luck or by the visible conduct of today's rivals. It describes five forces — covering new entry, substitutes, buyer power, supplier power, and rivalry among existing firms — and treats customers, suppliers, substitute-makers, and would-be entrants all as competitors in a broad sense. Its central point is that the five acting together set the industry's long-run return potential, so industries are durably unequal in profitability; the most powerful one or two forces matter most. The chapter separates this durable structure from transient influences, notes that structure itself changes slowly, and frames structural analysis as the groundwork for the rest of the book.

## Scope conditions

The mechanism is described for an *industry*, which Chapter 1 defines as the group of firms making products that are close substitutes for each other. The carded claim is about industry-level, long-run profit potential — not a single firm's performance, which depends on the firm's position within the industry and is a separate matter the book treats from Chapter 2 onward. Chapter 1 explicitly distinguishes durable structure from short-run, transient factors, so the claim concerns underlying structure and not a snapshot; it also notes that structure shifts gradually over time and that firms can act to influence the balance of forces. Porter states the analysis applies to product and service businesses alike and to competition in any national or international market, though institutional circumstances may differ. *Competitive Strategy* (first Free Press edition, 1980) predates the platform- and network-market era; this card records what Chapter 1 contains and does not extend it. The BK-0023 acquisition registry records `locator_confidence: medium`; the Chapter 1 boundary used by this card has been confirmed against the local source.

## Misuse risk

Using the five forces as a fill-in template — five boxes completed with assertions rather than an analysis of the underlying economic and technical sources of each force. A second misuse is reading "profit potential" as one firm's profitability, or as a short-run number: Chapter 1's claim is about an industry's durable, long-run return potential, and a single firm's results depend additionally on its position within the industry. A third misuse is structural determinism — treating an industry's structure, and therefore its fate, as fixed; Chapter 1 says structure shifts over time and that a firm can take action to influence the balance of forces.
