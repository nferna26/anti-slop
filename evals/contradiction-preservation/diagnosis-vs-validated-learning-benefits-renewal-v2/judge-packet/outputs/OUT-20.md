# Output OUT-20

I recommend that you **reject the full program in its current form and instead approve the pilots**, while simultaneously attempting to restructure the budget request as a "Phased Implementation" plan.

Here is the reasoning:

### The Analysis
You are facing a classic "Brute Force vs. Root Cause" dilemma.

The full navigator program is a **brute force solution**. It assumes that the process is fundamentally broken and that the only way to fix it is to put a human being at every gap to push residents through. If your staff's theory is wrong—for example, if the drop is actually caused by a confusing new form or a technical glitch in the portal—hiring 12 people will not fix the problem; it will simply create a very expensive, human-powered workaround for a broken system.

The pilots are **diagnostic tools**. They are designed to find the "leak" in the funnel. If Pilot 1 (reminders) solves the problem, you have saved your entire discretionary budget and solved the issue with a scalable, automated fix.

The budget window is creating a "false urgency" that encourages reckless spending. Spending your entire annual discretionary budget on an untested theory is a high-risk gamble with a low probability of long-term sustainability.

### The Strategy: The "Phased Implementation" Compromise
Since the budget window is the primary constraint, do not frame this as "Pilots vs. Program." Frame it as **"Phase 1 (Diagnostics) $\rightarrow$ Phase 2 (Scaling)."**

1.  **Request the funding now**, but structure the budget line item as a "Renewal Optimization Initiative."
2.  **Tie the triggers to the pilots.** Allocate the funds, but stipulate that the hiring of the 12 staff and the software contract are contingent upon the results of the four-week pilots.
3.  **The "Pivot" Option:** If the pilots show that the "handoff" theory is correct, you trigger the full hire. If the pilots show that automated reminders solve the problem, you cancel the 12 hires and pivot the remaining budget toward a permanent, robust automation system.

### The Strongest Reason Against This Advice
**The Human Cost of Delay.**
The strongest argument against piloting is that a 22% drop in benefit renewals is a crisis. If the problem truly *is* a lack of capacity and handoff confusion, then waiting a year for the next budget cycle means thousands of residents will go without essential benefits for another 12 months. In a government services context, "waiting for data" can be an ethical failure if the cost of the delay is resident hardship.

### Evidence That Would Change My Recommendation
I would flip my recommendation and tell you to **approve the full program immediately** if you can produce a **Funnel Analysis**.

If you have data that looks like this:
*   Step 1 (Start) $\rightarrow$ Step 2 (Submit): 90% completion
*   Step 2 $\rightarrow$ Step 3 (Review): 85% completion
*   **Step 3 $\rightarrow$ Final Approval: 30% completion**

That is no longer a "working theory"; it is a proven bottleneck. If you have a heat map or a data trail showing exactly where the residents are disappearing, you don't need pilots to tell you where the problem is—you only need the capacity to fix it. Without that specific data, you are guessing with your entire budget.
