# Output OUT-28

You should choose a **constrained version of both**: secure the funding now, but structure the implementation as a phased rollout contingent on the pilot results.

### The Recommendation: The "Conditional Commitment"
Do not gamble your entire discretionary budget on an untested theory, but do not let a bureaucratic window close on a critical service.

**The strategy:** Request the full budget allocation now to "lock in" the funds, but write the operational plan as a staggered implementation.

1.  **Secure the Budget:** Submit the request for the 12 staff and the software today. In government budgeting, "securing the funds" is often a separate act from "spending the funds."
2.  **Phase the Hires:** Instead of hiring 12 people on Day 1, hire 2 "Lead Navigators" immediately. This satisfies your deputy’s third pilot (the scaled-down navigator) while the other 10 positions remain "budgeted but vacant."
3.  **Modular Software:** Negotiate the software contract with a "pilot period" or a monthly subscription for the first 90 days, rather than a multi-year locked-in license.
4.  **Concurrent Testing:** Run the automated reminders and callback scheduler (Pilots 1 and 2) immediately. These are low-cost and can be implemented while the hiring process for the navigators is underway.

### Why this is the right move
If you go full-scale and the problem is actually "missing documents" (Pilot 1), you have 12 expensive staff members doing a job that a simple automated email could have solved. You’ve wasted your budget and created a permanent overhead cost.

If you run the pilots first and they prove the "handoff" theory, you have the evidence you need, but no money to fix it. You have effectively decided that the budget is more important than the residents who are losing their benefits for the next 12 months.

### The strongest reason against this
**The "Sunk Cost" Trap and Political Pressure.**
Once you have secured the budget and hired the first few people, there is immense organizational pressure to "just finish the rollout" regardless of what the pilots show. If the pilots suggest the problem is actually a simple software glitch or a document issue, it will be politically and administratively difficult to tell your team (and your superiors) that you are canceling the remaining 10 hires and the software contract. You risk becoming committed to a solution simply because you already started the engine.

### Evidence that would change this recommendation
I would change this recommendation to **"Approve the Full Program Now"** if you can produce a **Drop-off Report.**

If you can pull data showing exactly where the 22% gap occurs (e.g., "80% of drop-outs happen between Step 2 in Dept A and Step 3 in Dept B"), the "working theory" is no longer a guess—it is a fact. If the data proves the handoff is the bottleneck, the pilots are a waste of time; you should move straight to the solution.
