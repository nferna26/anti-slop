You are sitting on a well-known tension in the management literature, and it is worth naming the two schools, because your colleagues are each channeling one.

Your head of product is channeling Richard Rumelt's *strategy kernel*: good strategy starts with a diagnosis — you name what is critical about the challenge before you design a response. Diagnose first, then act.

Your founding engineer is channeling Eric Ries's *Lean Startup* and its principle of validated learning: under uncertainty you make progress by testing assumptions against real customer behaviour rather than by planning. Experiment first.

Both are respected frameworks, and they are less opposed than they look. The common reconciliation is to sequence them — a light pass of Rumelt's diagnosis to frame the bet, then Ries-style experiments to validate it. Here is how I would spend the quarter:

- Weeks 1–2: a focused diagnosis. Pull together what you know and define what you believe the patient-portal opportunity actually is and why it would work.
- Weeks 3–5: a Lean-Startup-style validation. Put an MVP — a landing page or a concierge pilot — in front of customers and measure real behaviour against that diagnosis.
- Weeks 6 onward: decide. If the experiments validate the bet, build it out; if not, revise the idea or drop it.

If I had to say which framework should lead, I would lean toward Ries. The Lean Startup method is purpose-built for your situation — a new product under real uncertainty — and its central teaching is not to over-invest in a plan before you have evidence. Rumelt's kernel is invaluable, but it does its best work when you are setting direction for a business you already understand well. For a brand-new product bet, validated learning is the safer master framework, with diagnosis as the supporting first step.

So: apply both, but let Lean Startup lead. Diagnose lightly, validate early, and let the evidence drive the rest of the quarter.
